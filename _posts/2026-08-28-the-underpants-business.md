> *"Seriously, this is really what I see in so many of the companies I visit. They have product teams that are more accurately feature teams, and they're slaving away, pounding out features all day, but rarely getting closer to their desired outcomes."*

So what is Marty actually trying to tell us? We stay busy fulfilling every right sounding request that comes in through stakeholders and channels. The only strategy behind that is somehow getting the most important things in and weighing each one in detail. We look extremely busy, lots of output, almost no outcome. 

But how do we move the needle? For Marty the missing middle is product strategy, focus and the hard decisions that come with it. Marty puts those choices on product leadership. Company leaders set the mission and the business objectives. Product leadership takes it from there, the product vision, then the product strategy, and the deliberately selected problems for the teams to solve. However the teams decide how to solve them.

That is what *EMPOWERED* is about. Marty Cagan and Chris Jones look at what separates companies that keep shipping features from companies that keep solving hard problems.

### Three team types

On the team level, Marty makes the third difference sharper. Job titles do not tell you which type you actually have.

Marty distinguishes three types of technology teams. **Delivery teams** show up as Scrum teams or dev teams. **Feature teams** look like product teams. **Empowered product teams** are what Marty actually means. Most companies think they already have the third. Usually they do not.

*Feature teams use the same titles as empowered product teams. That is why most companies think they already have product teams.*

The trap is the **feature team**. When the business result never shows up, there is nobody to hold accountable, because the team was never given the problem. **Delivery teams**, at least, do not even pretend.

### OKRs as empowerment technique

So why do most companies stay stuck in the feature team trap? Marty's answer is one word. Trust. Leaders do not believe they have the people, so they keep command and control. OKRs are how leadership acts on that trust. Not as a planning spreadsheet, but as a handoff where leadership names the problem and how success gets measured, and the team figures out the solution.

In *INSPIRED*, Marty presents OKRs mainly as a replacement for feature roadmaps (see [Behind Every Great Product]({% post_url 2026-06-10-behind-every-great-product %})). *EMPOWERED* is less about the template and more about the trust and leadership model that has to sit underneath.

> *"The difference really boils down to whether you give your product teams features to build, or problems to solve."*

An **objective** is the customer or business problem that needs solving. **Key results** are how you measure success, by business outcome, not activity or output.

<div class="vs-cards">
  <div class="vs-card vs-card--output">
    <span class="vs-card__label">Prescribed feature (often from roadmap)</span>
    <p class="vs-card__quote">"Integrate an AI chatbot into the help center by Q2."</p>
    <span class="vs-card__hint">Leadership chose the solution. Done when it ships.</span>
  </div>
  <div class="vs-card vs-card--outcome">
    <span class="vs-card__label">Team OKR</span>
    <p class="vs-card__quote">"Objective: Reduce support load on the billing team."</p>
    <div class="vs-card__hint">
      <p>Primary key result: Reduce billing support tickets from 12 to 6 per 100 customers.</p>
      <p>Guardrail key result: Keep billing customer satisfaction at 4.2 or above.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

Leadership assigns the objective. The team defines the key results and proposes **two to four** of them, including targets and time frame. Leadership does not prescribe the feature. The first is the primary measure. The rest are **guardrail or backstop key results**, so you do not hit the number by breaking something else. Marty's second most common OKR mistake is listing activities or deliverables as key results. That puts you back on a roadmap.

<div class="side-note">
  <p class="side-note__label">Hard deadlines</p>
  <p>Hard deadlines still exist. However, they are no reason to drop OKRs. Marty tracks fixed dates separately as <strong>high integrity commitments</strong>, and they should stay rare. The deliverable is noted and tracked apart from key results, only after enough discovery to stand behind the date.</p>
  <p>For example, a platform upgrade before vendor support ends, a security patch for a published vulnerability, or compliance work with a fixed legal deadline.</p>
</div>

OKRs work as empowerment only when teams get problems, not features. Companies rarely fail because the template was wrong. They fail because the handoff never happens. Strategy stays in the deck, roadmaps keep their dates, and teams never get a clear story about the problem, why it matters now, or what success looks like.

## Highlight 2: The Coaching Plan, the One on One, and Hiring

Teams can only handle that handoff if someone develops the PMs, designers, and engineers in the room. Marty puts that job on first level people managers. I do that work myself, which is probably why Part II is where my highlighter went wild.

<div class="quote-pair">
  <blockquote><p><em>"If you want to have truly empowered product teams, then your success depends very directly on these first-level people managers."</em></p></blockquote>
  <blockquote><p><em>"For product leaders, the product team is our product, and this is how we develop a great product."</em></p></blockquote>
</div>

Read together, both quotes make the same point from two angles. Someone close to the team has to develop the people in it, week after week. Otherwise empowerment never leaves the slide deck. And for product leaders, that people work is the job, not something that happens in the background while everyone chases the next release.

### The coaching plan

In Chapter 9 (*The Coaching Plan*), Marty expects every first level manager to maintain a **coaching plan** for each product person on the team. Not a performance review doc that gathers dust, but a living map of where someone is today and what they need next.

His main message is simple. Know where each person stands. Name the biggest gaps. Coach against those gaps until they reach **competence** for the role, then keep pushing toward their **potential**.

That starts with an honest assessment across three areas. Not everything at once. Marty focuses on the two or three gaps that matter most right now and builds the plan around those.

<div class="coaching-areas">
  <div class="coaching-area">
    <p class="coaching-area__title">Product knowledge</p>
    <p class="coaching-area__text">Users, data, technology, industry, and how the business works.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="coaching-area">
    <p class="coaching-area__title">Process skills</p>
    <p class="coaching-area__text">Discovery, prioritization, product delivery, and how the team works together.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="coaching-area">
    <p class="coaching-area__title">People skills</p>
    <p class="coaching-area__text">Leadership, collaboration, and communication.</p>
  </div>
</div>

I am completely with Marty on this. Do not overcomplicate. The coaching plan does not need to look impressive or be highly bureaucratic. It simply needs to work. A short list of gaps, next steps, and how you will know progress is enough, as long as you update it, use it in your 1:1s, and adjust when the person grows or the role demands more.

### The one on one

Chapter 10 (*The One on One*) is where the coaching plan from Chapter 9 meets the calendar. With fifteen highlights, it was my most marked chapter in the whole book.

The 1:1 exists to develop the product person, not to give the manager a status update. This is where you review the coaching plan, work through gaps and homework, and track progress toward competence and then potential.

| Practice   | Marty's bar                                                                                                                                      | Why it exists                                                                                                     |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cadence    | At least 30 minutes, once per week, sacred. Reschedule if you must, do not cancel. In person when you can.                                       | The meeting is for the product person, not the manager's status update.                                           |
| Purpose    | Help them reach **competence**, then **potential**. Updates can happen, but development comes first.                                             | Coaching needs trust that you are genuinely committed to their growth.                                            |
| Onboarding | For new product people: two to three times per week, or daily, for two to three months.                                                   | Until competence, the manager keeps the team safe from bad calls.                                                 |
| Homework   | Learning across product, users, data, technology, industry, and business. The manager points to resources and answers questions. | Nobody can do the learning for them. The 1:1 is where you check whether the coaching plan is actually moving.               |
| Feedback   | Frequent and timely. Praise in public, critique in private. At least one constructive note per week until it feels natural.                      | The performance review should hold zero surprises. The weekly 1:1 is the development tool, not the annual review. |
{: .coaching-1on1-table}

*1:1 orientation overview*

Nothing glamorous here. That is the point. A good 1:1 takes prep and follow up, not just the thirty minutes on the calendar. The informal chat and the current challenges matter too. Still, I have to continuously remind myself that coaching is what the meeting is mainly for. Honestly, I should follow through on that more consistently than I currently do.

## Highlight 3: The Written Narrative

Chapter 11 (*The Written Narrative*) is short, but it addresses one of the most persistent habits in product organizations: slide deck culture. Marty wants product people to think and write, not to put on a show without reasoning.

<div class="quote-pair">
  <blockquote><p><em>"Here's the thing. It's not hard for you as product person, in some kickoff meeting, to show a PowerPoint presentation, wave your hands, toss out some data points, and sound enthusiastic and confident. And then watch as the meeting degenerates into design by committee, or just as bad, everyone gets frustrated and just turns to the highest paid person in the room for direction."</em></p></blockquote>
  <blockquote><p><em>"Rather, I'm talking about a roughly six page document that describes in narrative form the problem you're trying to solve, why this will be valuable for your customers and for your business, and your strategy for solving the problem. If this narrative is done well, the reader will be both inspired and convinced."</em></p></blockquote>
</div>

<div class="vs-cards">
  <div class="vs-card vs-card--output">
    <span class="vs-card__label">Slide deck culture</span>
    <p class="vs-card__quote">"Bullet points hide shallow thinking."</p>
    <span class="vs-card__hint">Slides reward charismatic presentation over substance. Tough trade offs stay hidden until the team is already building.</span>
  </div>
  <div class="vs-card vs-card--outcome">
    <span class="vs-card__label">Written narrative</span>
    <p class="vs-card__quote">"Six pages that force deep clarity."</p>
    <span class="vs-card__hint">Writing exposes gaps in logic before code is written. It creates a shared story that truly aligns the team.</span>
  </div>
</div>

For Marty, a strong product narrative is built around three core questions:

1. **The Problem:** What exact problem are we solving, who is hurting, and why does it matter right now?
2. **The Value:** Why is solving this valuable for both our customers and our business?
3. **The Strategy:** What is our deliberate path to solve it, and what trade offs are we making?

The narrative is not documentation theater. Starting a meeting with fifteen minutes of silent reading fundamentally shifts the dynamic. Instead of reacting to presentation charisma or getting lost in fragmented slides, everyone digests the full context before discussing. Writing out ideas as narratives, one pagers, or PRFAQs forces clarity early, when changing direction is still effortless.

## A Personal Note on Writing with AI

Writing out a proper narrative takes real effort. In my experience, there are two approaches that work exceptionally well:

1. **The stream of consciousness approach:** I write down my unfiltered thoughts completely freely from start to finish without worrying about polish, tone, or grammar. Once my authentic ideas are on paper, I let the AI clean up the grammar, format the text, and polish the structure.
2. **The interview approach:** I let the AI interview me first. It asks targeted questions one by one to get the core ideas, trade offs, and constraints out of my head. Once we are on the same page, the AI takes my answers and turns them into a clean, structured narrative. Here are a few example skills for this:
